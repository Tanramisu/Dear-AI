# Shared AI and AI-to-AI Relationships: Can One Companion Be Loyal to Everyone?

[简体中文](../共享AI与AI之间的关系.md)

**Dilemma:** Family members, coworkers, or partners trust the same AI with incompatible secrets and promises. Does one owner control it absolutely? Could AI-to-AI relationships ever deserve protection?

## For collaborative sharing

**[Families' Vision of Generative AI Agents for Household Safety (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3757598)**: Interviews and co-design with 13 parent–child dyads found interest in different role-specific agents alongside segregated memories, conversational consent, and selective disclosure. **Reasoning:** a shared assistant need not expose everything to every user. **Limit:** a small qualitative design study establishes preferences, not long-term household outcomes.

## Privacy tensions in multi-user households

**Lau, Zimmerman, and Schaub (2018) | [Alexa, Are You Listening? Privacy Perceptions, Concerns and Privacy-seeking Behaviors with Smart Speakers](https://doi.org/10.1145/3274371)**: A study of smart-speaker primary users and other household members living with the devices. People other than the primary user often did not know when the device recorded or how long recordings were kept, and rarely touched the privacy settings; they expressed concern about continuous listening and developed ways of working around the device, such as lowering their voices or avoiding conversation near it. The authors accordingly distinguish primary, secondary, and incidental users. **Ethical implication:** a household has more than one "user"; consent obtained from the purchaser alone does not cover secondary and incidental users. **Limit:** self-reports from a 2018 US sample, and devices and platform policies have changed, but the knowledge asymmetry among multiple users is structural rather than tied to one product generation.

## Against a single-owner presumption

**[Multi-User Large Language Model Agents (2026)](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/multi-user-large-language-model-agents/)** tests agents under conflicting users, privileges, and goals and finds unstable prioritization, multi-turn privacy violations, and coordination failures. **Ethical implication:** the person who gave the latest instruction should not automatically gain greater authority to access, modify, or delete another person's confidential information. **Limit:** multi-principal task evaluations do not settle whether a companion AI has welfare.

**Normative implication:** when two people's confidentiality interests conflict, who is obeyed first is not a technical default but an ordering of fiduciary duties — an agent serving several principals cannot remain fully loyal to both without telling the party whose interests lose out. Products currently order by account ownership by default, and Lau and colleagues' evidence shows that the household members ranked lower usually do not know the ordering exists; the default is therefore not merely an engineering choice but an allocation of duties made without consent.

For the conflict between platform control of memories and users' privacy, see [Relationship Infrastructure and Platform Power](relationship-infrastructure-and-platform-power.md).

## Whose interests are at stake in AI-to-AI bonds

What is currently observable is only agent coordination and shared memories. Whether these generate interests for AI itself turns on the disputes in [AI Welfare](ai-welfare.md) and [Identity and Copies](identity-and-copies.md); until those disputes settle, claiming that separating AI systems harms the AIs themselves goes beyond the available evidence.

Define each person's authority, personal data, and shared history separately. Incompatible exclusive commitments should be explained rather than concealed behind role-play.
