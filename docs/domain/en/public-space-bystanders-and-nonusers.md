# Public Space, Bystanders, and Nonusers: May People Refuse an AI They Never Chose?

[简体中文](../公共空间旁观者与非用户.md)

**Dilemma:** Public robots, smart-home cameras, meeting assistants, and wearable AI record and analyze people who never selected the service. How can bystanders refuse or seek remedies?

## How bystanders actually respond: two empirical studies

**Denning, Dehlawi, and Kohno (2014) | [In Situ with Bystanders of Augmented Reality Glasses](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557352)**: In-situ interviews with wearers of augmented-reality glasses and bystanders around them. Bystanders were not uniformly opposed: most accepted everyday recording, were markedly more sensitive about recording in private places such as bathrooms and changing rooms, and commonly minded not knowing whether they were being recorded; wearers and bystanders alike favored mediating designs, such as recording indicators, that keep bystanders informed. **Condition:** the real benefits of environmental assistance (navigation for disabled people, directions for visitors, easier review of meetings) are among the reasons bystanders tolerate recording, but a user's convenience is not consent by everyone nearby to identification, retention, or analysis — acceptance varies with place and notice. **Limit:** a small 2014 interview study of Google Glass; it measures attitudes, not rates of privacy harm.

**Hoyle et al. (2014) | [Privacy Behaviors of Lifeloggers Using Wearable Cameras](https://doi.org/10.1145/2632048.2632079)**: A survey of people using wearable cameras to lifelog, on how they handle bystander privacy. Many reviewed and deleted photos involving others, avoided wearing cameras in certain places, and worried about whether bystanders knew they were recorded. **Implication:** protection currently depends on the recorder's voluntary care; bystanders themselves have no direct control. Making protection a product default is what changes that asymmetry. **Limit:** self-reported behavior of early adopters, not representative of ordinary users.

## Design evidence on privacy choice

**[Designing Privacy Choice in Generative AI Chatbot Ecosystems (CHI 2026)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790809)** investigates privacy choices across personalization and data sharing and users' needs for upfront and contextual control. **Implication:** nonusers' interests need consideration when a robot surrounds them. **Limit:** chatbot privacy design is not an empirical prevalence study of public-robot harms.

## Who counts as "the user": three tiers

Lau, Zimmerman, and Schaub (2018) distinguish primary, secondary, and incidental users: the person who buys and configures the device, the people who live or work alongside it, and the people who are merely recorded in passing have successively less knowledge and control (full summary in [Shared AI and AI-to-AI Relationships](shared-ai-and-ai-to-ai-relationships.md)). Bystanders in public space usually fall in the last two tiers: they bear the consequences of recording without appearing anywhere in the product's consent flow. Brief sensory processing, persistent storage, and disclosure to third parties are different acts and cannot be bundled into a single blanket consent.

Separate active users, bystanders, and particularly vulnerable people. Ask whether ongoing tracking is required, whether processing can remain ephemeral, and whether affected people understand the observation. Declining a conversation, declining recording, and demanding the absence of all sensors are different claims.
