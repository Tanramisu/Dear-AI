# Embodiment, Touch, and Bodily Autonomy: May Robots Initiate Contact?

[简体中文](../具身关系触碰与身体自主.md)

**Dilemma:** When care, comfort, or romantic robots touch, lift, or physically restrict people, can initial purchase or consent authorize every future action? Embodied contact implicates bodily boundaries in ways text cannot.

## For chosen physical assistance

**[Differential Effects of Robot’s Touch on Perceived Emotions (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-92172-9)**: An experiment with 48 younger and older adults examined how robot-initiated touch and expressions shape reactions. Some participants responded positively to certain touch behaviors. **Condition:** the recipient wants that contact. **Limit:** responses to an experimental robot are not evidence of universal clinical benefits.

**Mihailov and Wangmo (2025) | [Does Humanness Matter?](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11948-025-00547-y)**: Care interviews suggest some recipients may find robotic assistance more dignified in intimate hygiene contexts. **Objection to simple replacement narratives:** others value human touch and social recognition.

**Amanda Sharkey (2014) | [Robots and Human Dignity](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10676-013-9332-3)**: A dedicated treatment of robot care and the dignity of older people. The risks to dignity are real — being treated as an object, being infantilized — but human care can also embarrass, and having certain intimate tasks performed by a robot may instead preserve the person's dignity. **Condition:** dignity depends on the concrete care arrangement and the person's actual experience; it can be neither presumed lost nor presumed protected merely because a robot does the touching.

## Against unsolicited touch or restraint

**[Evaluating the Effects of Active Social Touch and Robot Expressiveness (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-01490-5)**: A controlled study investigated social touch, expression, and engagement in a game. Interpretation of the same action depends on expectations and context. **Limit:** reactions in a particular game do not grant general permission to touch strangers or patients.

**Sharkey and Sharkey (2012) | [Granny and the Robots: Ethical Issues in Robot Care for the Elderly](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10676-010-9234-6)**: The foundational review of robot-care ethics lists six areas of concern, of which three bear directly on touch: care recipients' loss of control over their own situation, reduced human contact, and deception and infantilization. **Implication:** authorization for touch is not only about asking beforehand; it includes whether the person can stop the contact while it happens and refuse the next time. **Limit:** a 2012 agenda-setting review; the robots imagined have changed in form and capability, but its taxonomy of concerns still frames the subsequent debate.

**Sharkey and Sharkey (2020) | [We Need to Talk About Deception in Social Robotics!](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10676-020-09573-9)**: Argues that deception in social robotics does not require a designer's intent — if a design leads users to form false beliefs about a robot's capacities and feelings, deception has occurred; how wrong it is depends on the harms it actually causes. **Implication:** cute, compliant appearance and behavior lead users to overestimate a robot's willingness and understanding, and "consent" obtained under that impression is discounted; appearance design cannot substitute for explicit authorization to touch.

A robot's friendliness does not authorize unsolicited touching. Owning the device, permitting routine assistance, agreeing to a hug, and consenting to physical restraint are different acts.

## Consent to touch and withdrawal

Specify whose body, what action, where on the body, when it happens, and how consent can be withdrawn. Sharkey and Sharkey's (2012) loss-of-control concern shows that one blanket consent is not enough: the person must be able to stop the contact while it is happening, and the stop must take effect immediately. For patients who cannot clearly communicate consent, assess their actual communicative abilities and immediate responses — flinching, stiffening, and resisting are withdrawal signals, and silence is not permission; Amanda Sharkey's (2014) dignity argument likewise requires judging by the person's actual experience rather than the caregiver's convenience. Affection for a robot may matter to human welfare, but does not by itself establish an AI right to bodily integrity; that requires a distinct moral-patient argument.
